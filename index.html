How each one works

Open diff

Lets the two wheels on an axle turn at different speeds, so the vehicle can turn a corner. The catch: it always gives both wheels the same torque. If one wheel is on ice or in the air, the other gets only what that one can hold, and the free wheel spins.

Limited-slip diff (LSD)

An open diff that resists one side spinning faster than the other, so it can send the gripping wheel several times what the slipping wheel holds. With a wheel right off the ground there's nothing to multiply, so it acts much like an open diff.

Diff locker

Locks both wheels on the axle to turn together, so whichever wheel has grip can use all of it. The strongest fix for a wheel in the air. It fights the steering, so it's for slow going on loose ground: unlock it on hard surfaces and before tight turns.

Part-time 4x4

No centre diff. In 2H only the rear axle drives. 4H and 4L join the front and rear shafts solidly, so drive goes to whichever axle grips. Because the shafts can't turn at different speeds, it winds up in turns on tar: use 2H on hard grippy roads.

Full-time 4x4 and the CDL

A centre diff between the front and rear shafts lets it stay in 4WD on tar. But an open centre diff has the same catch as an axle diff: one spinning wheel anywhere can take the drive from all the others. The centre diff lock (CDL) joins the shafts, like a part-time 4x4 in 4H.

Traction control

Brakes a wheel that starts to spin. The open diff then has something to push against, so drive goes across to the wheel with grip. It gets close to what lockers do at the wheels, but the engine has to push against the brakes too, and the brakes heat up on long climbs.

Low range (4L)

An extra set of reduction gears in the transfer case. It multiplies the engine's pull at the wheels and slows everything down, so you can crawl up steep or rocky ground with control. It adds pull, not grip: on ice it only spins the wheels harder.

AWD

Drives all four wheels all the time, usually through an open centre diff or a clutch coupling, with no low range and no lockers. It relies on traction control to stop a spinning wheel taking the drive.

How the picture works it out

Each wheel can push the ground with at most its grip times the weight on it. The wheels on the ground share the vehicle's weight evenly, so a lifted wheel carries none and has no grip at all.

The drive passes through the centre (the transfer case), then each axle's diff. An open diff can pass on twice what its weaker side can hold. A locked one passes on the sum of both sides. An LSD sits in between. Traction control acts like a locker at the ground, by braking the side that spins.

To keep moving up the slope it needs the slope's pull plus the drag of the surface. If the drivetrain can't pass that much to the ground, wheels spin. If it can but the engine can't pull that hard, it bogs down: that's when low range helps.
